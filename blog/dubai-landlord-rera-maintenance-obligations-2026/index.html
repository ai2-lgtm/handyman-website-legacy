Key Takeaways

  • RERA Law 26 of 2007 places primary responsibility for major maintenance and structural repairs on the landlord, ensuring the property remains fit for purpose.
  • Tenancy contracts must clearly define the scope of maintenance duties for both parties, particularly distinguishing between routine upkeep and fundamental system repairs.
  • Thorough move-in and move-out inspections with detailed documentation are critical for landlords to prevent security deposit disputes regarding tenant-caused damage versus normal wear and tear.

Introduction: The Unseen Costs of Neglect

Imagine a landlord in a sleek Business Bay high-rise, meticulously calculating rental yields and property appreciation. They’ve just signed a new lease, confident in their investment. Three months later, a panicked call arrives: the AC in the master bedroom has completely failed, and it's 45°C outside. The tenant, unfamiliar with Dubai's stringent rental laws, is demanding immediate action. The landlord, equally unfamiliar with the nuances of RERA, initially dismisses it as a "tenant problem." This scenario, playing out in apartments across Dubai Marina, JVC, and Downtown Dubai, highlights a critical, often misunderstood aspect of property ownership: the landlord's maintenance obligations.

In a competitive market like Dubai, where property values and rental incomes are significant, understanding and fulfilling these duties isn't just about compliance; it's about tenant retention, protecting asset value, and avoiding costly legal battles. Neglect can lead to rapid deterioration of property infrastructure, especially with Dubai's extreme climate accelerating wear and tear on systems like air conditioning and plumbing. So, what exactly does the law expect from landlords in 2026, and how can they navigate these responsibilities effectively?

The RERA Framework: Law No. 26 of 2007

At the heart of Dubai's rental landscape lies Law No. 26 of 2007 Regulating the Relationship between Landlords and Tenants in the Emirate of Dubai (as amended by Law No. 33 of 2008), commonly known as the RERA Rental Law. This foundational legislation provides the legal framework governing tenancy agreements, outlining the rights and responsibilities of both parties. For maintenance, Article 16 is particularly salient:

"Unless otherwise agreed by the parties, the landlord shall, during the lease term, be responsible for the real property maintenance works and for repairing any defects or damages that may affect the tenant’s intended use of the real property."

This article is the cornerstone. It establishes a default position: landlords are responsible for maintenance. The crucial phrase, "Unless otherwise agreed by the parties," allows for some contractual deviation, but this deviation must be clear, explicit, and reasonable within the context of ensuring the property remains habitable and fit for purpose. It does not permit a landlord to entirely shirk fundamental responsibilities.

Unpacking Landlord Responsibilities: What Must Be Fixed?

So, what falls under the umbrella of "real property maintenance works" and "defects or damages that may affect the tenant’s intended use"? Here’s a breakdown of common areas where landlords typically bear the primary responsibility:

Structural Integrity and Core Systems

This is non-negotiable. Landlords are responsible for maintaining the structural soundness of the property. This includes walls, roofs, foundations, and load-bearing elements. Any major cracks, leaks from the roof, or foundational issues that compromise safety or habitability are unequivocally the landlord's problem. Imagine a villa in Arabian Ranches where a structural wall develops a significant crack – this is a landlord's immediate concern, requiring expert assessment and repair.

Air Conditioning Systems: A Dubai Imperative

In Dubai's relentless heat, a functioning AC system isn't a luxury; it's a necessity. Landlords are generally responsible for ensuring the AC units are in good working order at the commencement of the tenancy and throughout its duration. This includes major repairs to the compressor, evaporator coil, condenser, and refrigerant lines. When the outdoor condenser unit in a Palm Jumeirah villa fails due to the immense thermal load, necessitating a full unit replacement, that cost typically falls to the landlord. While tenancy agreements sometimes stipulate that tenants cover routine filter cleaning or minor servicing (e.g., re-gassing if no leak is present), any fundamental breakdown or systemic failure is the landlord's burden. The intense salt air corrosion common in coastal areas like Dubai Marina or JBR often accelerates the degradation of external AC components, making regular, professional AC maintenance crucial for landlords.

Plumbing and Drainage

Functional plumbing is another absolute requirement for habitability. Landlords are responsible for the main water supply lines, drainage systems, water heaters, and sanitary ware (toilets, sinks, showers) unless damage is clearly caused by tenant misuse. A burst pipe behind a wall, a perpetually leaking water heater, or a blocked main drain line (not due to tenant negligence like flushing inappropriate items) falls to the landlord. The calcification from hard water, common in Dubai, can lead to premature failure of pipes and water heaters, requiring landlords to address these systemic issues.

Electrical Systems and Safety

Ensuring a safe and functional electrical system is paramount. Landlords are responsible for the main electrical wiring, distribution boards, switches, sockets, and light fixtures. Faulty wiring, tripping circuit breakers due to an overloaded grid (not tenant appliances), or non-functional essential lighting are the landlord's responsibility. Regular electrical inspections can preempt issues, especially in older properties where wiring might not meet current DEWA load calculations. The consistent operation of the earth leakage circuit breaker (ELCB) is critical for safety, and its malfunction is a landlord's problem to solve.

Built-in Appliances

If the property is rented with built-in appliances (e.g., oven, hob, dishwasher, washing machine, refrigerator), the landlord is generally responsible for their repair or replacement if they break down due to normal use. It's crucial for the tenancy contract to explicitly list which appliances are included and the landlord's commitment to their upkeep. Without such clarity, disputes often arise.

Common Areas and External Structures

For apartments, maintenance of common areas (corridors, lobbies, gyms, pools) is typically managed by the building's Owners' Association, with costs covered by service charges. However, issues directly impacting the rented unit, such as leaks from the ceiling originating from the floor above, are usually the landlord's responsibility to address with the Owners' Association. For villas, external structures like boundary walls, gates, and the property's exterior paintwork are the landlord's domain. The relentless sun and humidity in Dubai demand a repaint every few years, which is a landlord obligation.

Tenant Responsibilities: Routine Upkeep and Damage

While landlords bear the brunt of major maintenance, tenants are not without their duties. Article 16 allows for "otherwise agreed," and most tenancy contracts specify minor maintenance up to a certain monetary threshold per incident. This typically covers:

  • Minor repairs: Replacing lightbulbs, fixing a loose cabinet hinge, unblocking a minor sink drain.
  • Routine cleaning: Maintaining the cleanliness of the property.
  • Preventing damage: Using the property responsibly and preventing damage.
  • Damage caused by negligence: If a tenant breaks a window, floods the bathroom due to an overflowing tub, or damages an appliance through misuse, they are responsible for the repair or replacement.
  • Normal wear and tear vs. damage: This is a key distinction. "Normal wear and tear" (e.g., faded paint, worn carpet, minor scuffs) is the landlord's responsibility. "Damage" (e.g., holes in walls, broken tiles, severe stains) is the tenant's.

For example, if a tenant living in a JVC apartment accidentally drills through a water pipe while hanging a picture, that repair is on them. However, if the water heater in the same apartment simply stops working after five years of normal use, that's a landlord issue.

Preventing Deposit Disputes: The Power of Documentation

The security deposit is a frequent source of contention. Landlords are permitted to deduct costs for damages caused by the tenant beyond normal wear and tear. To avoid disputes, landlords must:

  1. Thorough Move-In Inspection: Conduct a detailed inspection with the tenant before they move in. Document every existing flaw, no matter how minor, with timestamped photos and videos. Have the tenant sign off on this report.
  2. Clear Tenancy Contract: Ensure the tenancy contract explicitly outlines maintenance responsibilities, detailing what constitutes minor vs. major repairs, who is responsible for specific appliances, and any monetary thresholds.
  3. Regular Communication: Encourage tenants to report issues promptly. Respond to maintenance requests in a timely manner.
  4. Move-Out Inspection: Repeat the detailed inspection process upon tenant vacating, comparing the property's condition against the move-in report. Any new damage (beyond normal wear and tear) should be documented thoroughly.
  5. Maintain Records: Keep meticulous records of all maintenance requests, repairs undertaken, and associated costs.

This rigorous approach protects both parties. It provides the landlord with evidence if deductions are necessary and protects the tenant from unfair charges for pre-existing conditions or normal wear and tear.

When Things Go Wrong: Legal Recourse and Practical Steps

If a landlord fails to address a critical maintenance issue that impacts the tenant's enjoyment or safety of the property, the tenant has recourse. The first step is usually formal written communication, detailing the issue and requesting action within a reasonable timeframe (often 7-14 days for non-emergencies, less for urgent matters). If the landlord remains unresponsive, the tenant can escalate the matter to the Rental Disputes Center (RDC), a judicial arm of the Dubai Land Department. The RDC will mediate and, if necessary, issue a binding judgment. Landlords facing such a situation should be prepared to present evidence of their efforts, communications, and any contractual clauses. Ignoring an RDC order can lead to significant penalties.

Proactive Maintenance: The Smart Landlord's Strategy

For landlords, especially those with multiple properties or high-value assets in areas like Emirates Hills, a proactive maintenance strategy isn't an expense; it's an investment. Regular preventative maintenance can:

  • Extend Asset Lifespan: Routine AC servicing, plumbing checks for leaks, and electrical safety inspections prevent minor issues from becoming catastrophic failures. For instance, a small, unnoticed water leak can lead to extensive water damage and even structural compromise if left untreated.
  • Reduce Emergency Repairs: Addressing issues before they escalate minimizes costly, disruptive emergency call-outs. Who wants a burst pipe at 2 AM in Jumeirah Islands?
  • Enhance Tenant Satisfaction: A well-maintained property and responsive landlord lead to happier tenants, higher retention rates, and fewer void periods.
  • Preserve Property Value: Regular upkeep ensures the property remains in excellent condition, protecting its market value and rental yield.
  • Ensure Compliance: Proactive checks ensure all systems meet safety standards and RERA requirements.
